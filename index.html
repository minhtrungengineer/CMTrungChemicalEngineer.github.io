01 · About the Researcher

Designing the Quantum Architecture of Clean Energy

I am a materials science researcher focusing on hydrogen storage, MOF–aerogel composite materials, adsorption–diffusion dynamics, and quantum tunneling effects at cryogenic temperatures.

My research integrates computational modeling, thermodynamics, and nanomaterial engineering to architect the next generation of clean-energy systems — where hydrogen atoms traverse energy barriers not by classical motion, but by the quiet elegance of quantum mechanics.

Working at the Advanced Material Laboratory at Can Tho University, I combine density functional theory, molecular dynamics, and multiscale simulation to decode the behavior of hydrogen inside nanoporous hybrid frameworks at temperatures approaching absolute zero.

02 · Research Overview

Core Focus: Quantum Materials for Hydrogen

An integrated investigation spanning tunneling phenomena, adsorption thermodynamics, and multiscale modeling of hybrid nanoporous composites engineered for cryogenic energy storage.

01 / MECHANISMS

Quantum Tunneling

Analysis of hydrogen tunneling probabilities across sub-nanometer energy barriers within MOF pore channels at cryogenic temperatures.

02 / SORPTION

Hydrogen Adsorption

Quantifying physisorption isotherms, binding energies, and site-specific uptake in metal–organic framework architectures.

03 / KINETICS

Diffusion Dynamics

Molecular transport pathways, mean-square displacement, and anomalous diffusion within hierarchical pore networks.

04 / HYBRID

MOF–Aerogel Systems

Designing composite hierarchies that merge crystalline MOF order with aerogel mesoporosity for optimal storage density.

05 / THERMODYNAMICS

Cryogenic Physics

Investigating thermal behavior, isosteric heats, and entropy-driven effects near liquid nitrogen and helium regimes.

06 / COMPUTATIONAL

Material Modeling

DFT, MD, and GCMC simulations bridging quantum and mesoscopic scales for predictive materials design.

06 · Quantum Tunneling Visualization

When Particles Pass Through Walls

In classical mechanics, a hydrogen atom with kinetic energy below a potential barrier is reflected. In the quantum regime, the same atom carries a finite probability of appearing on the other side — a phenomenon known as quantum tunneling.

At cryogenic temperatures, where thermal hopping is frozen, tunneling becomes the dominant transport channel. My work quantifies this contribution inside MOF–aerogel hybrid pores, revealing regimes where classical diffusion models break down.

The visualization opposite depicts a wave packet encountering a rectangular barrier: part is reflected, but a non-zero amplitude transmits — the essence of tunneling probability and the engine of low-temperature hydrogen mobility.
